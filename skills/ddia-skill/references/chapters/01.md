# Chapter 1. Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture

Original applied guidance, not copied book text. These procedures are engineering deductions; retrieve the cited source sections before attributing a consequential claim to DDIA. Verify product/version guarantees independently.

## Source orientation

- Choose systems from the workload and users: OLTP typically needs many small, low-latency point reads and mutations; analytics scans and aggregates large histories and often permits ad-hoc queries. The same SQL interface does not imply the same storage or operational needs. Separate analytics when resource contention, access boundaries, integration, or query shape justify it; HTAP does not remove those organizational distinctions.
- Name the system of record and derived representations explicitly. A derived cache, index, materialized view, or trained model can be recreated from authoritative input, but its value depends on keeping the derivation up to date. The role depends on application use, not the database brand.
- Cloud versus self-hosting depends on staff expertise, variable versus predictable demand, customization, service visibility, vendor switching cost, data trust and location, and total operation cost. Managed services shift operations toward integration, quotas, cost, and diagnosis; they do not eliminate it.
- Distribution is justified by inherent communication, resource limits, fault tolerance, geography, elasticity, hardware specialization, or constraints on location. It adds ambiguous RPC outcomes, network cost, diagnosis difficulty, and cross-service consistency work. A single machine can be simpler, cheaper, and faster when it meets actual needs.
- Microservices primarily enable team independence and separate deployment or resource allocation. Small teams can incur disproportionate testing, API-evolution, deployment, and observability overhead. Serverless billing and allocation still have execution and startup constraints.
- Storage decisions include privacy, deletion, liability and human consequences. Immutable logs and derived datasets complicate deletion. Data minimization can be a rational engineering choice; the chapter does not establish a universally compliant architecture.

## Operational and analytical workloads

Concept: `workload-separation`.

**Apply when:** Reports contend with customer writes, or a warehouse/lake is proposed.

**Procedure:** Inventory point lookups, mutations, scans, data age and ad-hoc joins. Measure shared CPU, I/O and connection contention; compare query/index or scheduling changes before introducing a second platform. Include ETL ownership and movement cost.

**Qualifications:** SQL support does not make two engines operationally interchangeable. A warehouse is not required merely because a dashboard exists.

**Incorrect example:** A 200-row daily report is moved to a distributed lake solely because it is analytics.

**Sufficient example:** Run the report on the existing database first; isolate analytics when measured interference or access requirements justify it.

**Falsification check:** Run representative reports during peak writes and compare customer latency, cost and freshness.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_analytics`, `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_dwh`.

## Systems of record and rebuildable state

Concept: `authority-derived`.

**Apply when:** A cache, search index, feature store or second database is introduced.

**Procedure:** Draw each authoritative fact and every derived copy. Identify input history, transformation version, checkpoint and deletion path needed to rebuild each copy. Assign one accountable owner for inconsistency and recovery.

**Qualifications:** A copy is derived only if required state can actually be reconstructed. Logs that expire before recovery invalidate that assumption.

**Incorrect example:** A search index accepts the only copy of a customer correction, while the database is called authoritative.

**Sufficient example:** Persist the correction to the system of record and derive the index, with a repair route and freshness objective.

**Falsification check:** Delete an isolated projection and rebuild it from retained inputs; compare business facts and deletion results.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_derived`, `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_compliance`.

## Managed services and storage/compute separation

Concept: `cloud-operations`.

**Apply when:** Choose cloud versus self-hosting or independently scale storage and compute.

**Procedure:** Compare staffing, on-call capability, quotas, demand variation, data location, egress and exit paths over a realistic time horizon. Trace object-store and network dependence through the supposed compute-independent architecture.

**Qualifications:** Managed means different responsibilities, including integration, access, recovery, quotas and bills. Vendor claims are not configuration guarantees.

**Incorrect example:** Choose a managed warehouse on compute price alone, ignoring scans, transfer and operational ownership.

**Sufficient example:** Estimate representative end-to-end cost and recovery; choose managed operation when its concrete burden and benefits fit the team.

**Falsification check:** Exercise quota exhaustion and restore with a test dataset; measure a representative bill and export path.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_cloud_tradeoffs`, `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_storage_compute`.

## Single-node versus distributed architecture

Concept: `distribution-necessity`.

**Apply when:** A queue, cluster, cache or distributed database is proposed for growth.

**Procedure:** Identify the unmet capacity, availability, geographic or ownership requirement. Establish a single-node baseline and a credible growth trigger. Account for new failure boundaries, data copies and consistency work.

**Qualifications:** A small deployment can still need geographic resilience; a high row count alone does not prove distribution is necessary.

**Incorrect example:** A service with ten writes a minute gets five services and consensus because traffic might grow.

**Sufficient example:** Start with one service and sufficient durable storage; document the observed bottleneck or recovery requirement that would justify change.

**Falsification check:** Load the simple baseline with realistic skew and a failure scenario; test the requirement rather than a component count.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_distributed`, `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_dist_sys_problems`.

## Microservices, serverless and team boundaries

Concept: `service-boundaries`.

**Apply when:** Split an application or choose serverless execution.

**Procedure:** Map independent teams, releases, state ownership and resource shapes. Estimate deployment, API compatibility, tracing and incident work. Check startup latency, execution duration and concurrency limits for serverless tasks.

**Qualifications:** Service boundaries are organizational and operational choices; they do not automatically create isolation or durability.

**Incorrect example:** Split a two-person application into services sharing one writable schema and synchronous request chain.

**Sufficient example:** Keep a modular application unless independent ownership or scaling offsets coordination cost; isolate a clearly owned worker when justified.

**Falsification check:** Trace one release and one dependency failure end to end, including rollback and operator actions.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_microservices`, `OEBPS/ch01.html#sec_introduction_operations`.
