# Chapter 2. Defining Nonfunctional Requirements

Original applied guidance, not copied book text. These procedures are engineering deductions; retrieve the cited source sections before attributing a consequential claim to DDIA. Verify product/version guarantees independently.

## Source orientation

- Performance has at least response time and throughput dimensions. Client response time includes service time, network delays, and queues; measuring only service time hides user-visible delays. Tail latency matters especially when one request fans out to multiple dependencies and waits for the slowest.
- Use distributions and explicit service objectives rather than an average alone. The chapter warns that averaging percentiles is mathematically invalid; combine histograms. Choose targets according to user impact and cost, not an arbitrary pursuit of more nines.
- The timeline case compares read-time joins with materialization. Precomputation trades cheaper reads for fan-out writes; heavy outliers such as celebrities may need a hybrid. Average follower count alone is insufficient to size or choose the design.
- Faults concern parts; failure concerns the system failing to meet the required service. Fault tolerance must state which fault types and quantities are tolerated. Replicas cannot fix every fault: software defects and configuration failures can be highly correlated.
- Overload may become metastable: queueing causes timeouts, retries amplify load, and the system may not recover just because demand drops. Backoff with jitter, circuit breakers, bounded retry admission, load shedding, and backpressure address different parts of this feedback loop.
- Scalability asks how particular load dimensions grow and what extra resources maintain performance. Early products should keep flexibility and avoid speculative scale; systems often need redesign at an order-of-magnitude growth rather than a universal scaling architecture.
- Maintainability includes operability, simplicity, and evolvability. Automation has edge cases and can make exceptional recovery harder; a useful balance includes clear manual controls. Reversible migrations reduce change risk. Blameless incident analysis should expose systemic priorities and assumptions rather than assign personal blame.

## Workload, skew and materialization

Concept: `load-shape`.

**Apply when:** Size a system or choose read-time joins versus precomputation.

**Procedure:** Specify peak rates, object sizes, cardinality, tenant skew and fan-out distributions. Compare read work against write amplification and rebuild work; model exceptional users separately from averages.

**Qualifications:** Averages conceal hot tenants and celebrities. Growth in one dimension may require a different design from growth in another.

**Incorrect example:** Materialize a timeline for every follower using average follower count, then overload on one celebrity post.

**Sufficient example:** Measure the distribution and consider a hybrid read/write strategy for the exceptional fan-out.

**Falsification check:** Replay skewed traffic including the largest tenant; observe queues and catch-up time.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch02.html#sec_introduction_materializing`, `OEBPS/ch02.html#id33`.

## Latency distributions and useful objectives

Concept: `performance-objectives`.

**Apply when:** Set or interpret performance objectives.

**Procedure:** Define the user operation, success/error population, observation point, percentile window and throughput. Combine histograms or raw observations before calculating percentiles; include dependency fan-out and queue time.

**Qualifications:** Service time is not client response time. Averaging percentiles does not yield the percentile of the combined workload.

**Incorrect example:** Average each host p99 and announce a global p99 target was met.

**Sufficient example:** Merge compatible latency observations/histograms and report the chosen percentile at the client boundary.

**Falsification check:** Use unequal traffic volumes and a slow shard to check that aggregation exposes rather than conceals tail latency.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch02.html#sec_introduction_percentiles`, `OEBPS/ch02.html#sidebar_percentiles`.

## Overload, retry amplification and backpressure

Concept: `overload`.

**Apply when:** Retries, unbounded queues or unstable recovery appear under load.

**Procedure:** Trace offered load through queues, pools, deadlines and retries. Bound work admission, propagate cancellation, separate retryable from ambiguous effects and assign retry budgets. Test recovery after load falls, not only peak throughput.

**Qualifications:** Backoff is not admission control; circuit breakers, shedding and backpressure solve different boundaries. Retrying may duplicate effects.

**Incorrect example:** Every layer retries three times while a saturated downstream queue has no bound.

**Sufficient example:** Bound concurrent work and retries, reject or defer excess deliberately, and preserve operation identity where retries are safe.

**Falsification check:** Drive sustained overload, remove excess demand, and verify queue drainage, bounded memory and renewed useful throughput.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch02.html#sidebar_metastable`, `OEBPS/ch02.html#sec_introduction_slo_sla`.

## Fault models and recovery objectives

Concept: `reliability-recovery`.

**Apply when:** Specify availability, redundancy, backups or recovery.

**Procedure:** Name tolerated faults and user-visible failure. Separate data-loss tolerance from outage duration; include correlated software/configuration errors and human recovery. Restore into isolation and reconcile lost or ambiguous commitments before reopening writes.

**Qualifications:** Replicas can faithfully reproduce corruption or deletion. Existing backups do not demonstrate achievable RPO/RTO.

**Incorrect example:** Declare disaster recovery complete because three replicas are healthy.

**Sufficient example:** Set recovery-point/time objectives, retain independent recoverable history and measure restoration plus reconciliation.

**Falsification check:** Delete or corrupt a test copy and restore to a chosen point; measure data gap, elapsed time and reopened-business invariants.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch02.html#sec_introduction_reliability`, `OEBPS/ch02.html#sec_introduction_hardware_faults`.

## Operability, simplicity and evolvability

Concept: `maintainability`.

**Apply when:** An architecture or automation reduces apparent coding effort but complicates operation.

**Procedure:** List ordinary and exceptional operator tasks, visibility, manual controls and rollback feasibility. Compare cognitive cost and staffing with the supported business requirement. Design changes so intermediate states are observable and recoverable.

**Qualifications:** Automation has failure modes; a human fallback must be real and documented. Simplicity is not minimum line count.

**Incorrect example:** A self-healing job silently retries forever and hides every exception from operators.

**Sufficient example:** Provide bounded retries, observable progress, actionable failure state and a controlled repair path.

**Falsification check:** Give another maintainer a partial failure and the runbook; observe whether they can diagnose and recover safely.

**Source sections:** `OEBPS/ch02.html#sec_introduction_maintainability`, `OEBPS/ch02.html#sec_introduction_evolvability`.
